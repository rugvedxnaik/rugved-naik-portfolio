Collectibility Over Loyalty

A dossier on why fragrance customers were collecting, not converting.

Status: routed, academic validated, finished dossier.

Role

Academic market-reading project.

Problem

Retention language risked confusing collectors with loyalists.

Evidence

4,700+ reviews, competitive benchmarking, attribute mining and social listening.

Decision

Separate object value from identity value before deciding product and GTM moves.

Artifact

Identity Value x Object Value framework.

Outcome

Clearer strategy for collectibility, drops and identity attachment.

The signal

I do not stay loyal to a fragrance. I build a collection that says who I am becoming.

Why this needed evidence, not intuition

Fragrance brands often lean on a comfortable story: find your signature scent, wear it forever, become known for it. That story is easy to build around because it turns repeat purchase into loyalty and loyalty into identity.

The early signal suggested something more specific. People seemed attached to the object, the display, and the ritual of choosing, but that did not prove the scent had become a durable part of the self. The only way to separate those forms of attachment was to read the behavior at scale.

  • More than 4,700 reviews were mined for attribute-level language, not only star ratings.
  • Competitive benchmarks checked whether the pattern was specific or category-wide.
  • Social listening across Instagram and TikTok captured shelfies, collection tours, and display behavior.

The evidence showed strong visibility and satisfaction signals. People liked the product and liked being seen with it. The weaker language sat around identity: confidence, attraction, self-recognition, and signature attachment.

The tension underneath the data

Object value and identity value are not opposites. They often coexist, which is why ordinary retention metrics flatten them into one thing: brand love. But the behaviors are different. A collector wants more objects. A loyalist wants the same one again.

If a collector base is managed with a loyalist playbook, the system rewards the wrong behavior. It pushes repeat purchase of one item when the stronger desire is range, display, completion, and the ability to keep adding to the set.

The translation: Identity Value x Object Value

The framework separates attachment to the product as a thing from attachment to the product as a statement about the self. Object Value covers the bottle, packaging, shelf appeal, collectibility, and social display. Identity Value covers confidence, attraction, signature ritual, and self-recognition.

Plotted against this framework, the customer language skewed toward high Object Value and moderate-to-low Identity Value. That made the strategic move clear: build for the collection behavior that was already present, then connect it to a sharper identity territory.

Output

Product implication: make the collection itself the product, with distinct releases, visible object codes, shelf-worthy packaging, and a reason to keep expanding the set.

GTM implication: move beyond "find your signature scent" and frame the shelf, the range, and the act of curation as the reason to return.

What it shows

This is the archive's clearest end-to-end case: a vague commercial hunch tested against real consumer evidence, turned into a simple framework, then translated into product and go-to-market decisions. It is finished enough to carry weight, but still useful because the framework can travel beyond this one fragrance case.
