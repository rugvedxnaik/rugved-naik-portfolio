Claim audit / Food and beverage / Proof systems

Claim Saturation

A dossier on what happens when every brand says the same thing.

Status: still routing, this is an active inquiry. Claim convergence is established, while the replacement signal is still being tested.

Disclosure: This independent analysis was conducted before and outside my Danone Innovation & Productivity PM internship. It uses only publicly visible category and claim language, and is unrelated to internal Danone work.

The signal

I want health benefits, but every claim sounds the same.

Why this needed a real audit, not a claim-fatigue hunch

It would be easy to write a confident line about claim fatigue and call it a consumer insight. That is the trap. Comfortable explanations are the ones that most need evidence, because they are easiest to repeat without checking.

The starting question was not whether health benefits matter. It was whether the same benefits had become so common that they stopped helping people choose between brands.

  • Claim language was audited across dairy and plant-based category pages.
  • Overlap was tracked across competing brands in the same shelf set and search context.
  • Specificity, proof format, delivery context, and visible trust cues were compared.

The audit confirmed that top benefit language often repeats with little differentiation in wording, framing, or proof. High protein, gut health, no added sugar, clean label, and better-for-you signals can be true and still stop working as differentiators.

The tension this raises, and has not fully resolved yet

Proving saturation is the easier half of the work. The harder half is deciding what carries weight once the obvious claim no longer separates one brand from another.

The unresolved tension is whether specificity cuts through, whether proof format matters more than claim content, or whether the strongest signal sits outside the claim entirely, in routine, packaging trust, repeat purchase, or willingness to pay.

Where this is heading, tentatively

The working hypothesis is that differentiation is shifting from the claim itself to the credibility structure around it. The useful question becomes not "what does the pack say" but "what makes this version of the claim harder to copy."

That direction is still tentative. The evidence supports the saturation read, but the replacement system needs more checking before it becomes a finished framework.

Output

Product implication (tentative): invest in proof infrastructure around claims, not only another benefit line on the package.

GTM implication (tentative): communicate specificity, verification, and routine fit where category-standard health language has become table stakes.

What it shows

This case shows restraint as part of the method. The archive does not need every signal to look finished. It needs each signal to be honest about what has been proven, what is only a working hypothesis, and what evidence still needs to be gathered.
